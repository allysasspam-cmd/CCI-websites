PERSONAL STORY · YOUTH MENTAL HEALTH

Mental health within Kenyan youth: Why are we still so silent?

When I was younger, I suffered from a severe form of allergic rhinitis. My symptoms made my eyes watery and my nose runny, and the children around me teased me for my noisy breathing and teary eyes. I had few friends and often spent time alone.

At the age of nine, I received surgery that helped me breathe and sleep better. My eyes stopped watering, and the bullying finally stopped. But while the surgery addressed my physical health, it did not address the emotional impact bullying had left on my mental health.

No one talked about mental health. I did not know how to express what I was feeling, whether I would be taken seriously, or what I could do about it. The physical symptoms became easier to manage, but the emotional experience was not something I knew how to explain.

Why does this conversation matter?

Mental health is part of overall wellbeing. It can shape how young people learn, build relationships, respond to challenges and see themselves. Yet a young person may stay quiet because they fear being judged, misunderstood or dismissed.

Bullying should not be brushed off as something a child simply has to get over. Even when a difficult situation ends, its emotional effects may continue. Listening without judgment and taking young people's experiences seriously can help them feel less alone.

What CCI hopes to change

The Compassionate Children's Initiative (CCI) aims to make youth mental health more visible in Kenya through awareness, interactive education and conversations that challenge stigma. We want young people to have language for what they are experiencing, know where they can seek support, and feel safe speaking up.

Our vision is a future where mental wellbeing is part of adolescent education and every young person knows that their experience matters. No one should have to struggle in silence simply because they do not know how to begin the conversation.

A gentle reminder: This article shares a personal experience and general awareness information. It is not a diagnosis or a substitute for professional mental health support. If you feel unsafe or overwhelmed, reach out to a trusted adult, school counsellor or qualified health professional.
SCHOOL WELLBEING

How to build a more supportive school community

A school community can make a difference by treating students with respect, responding to bullying and making it clear where students can turn for help.

Start with small actions: listen when someone shares a concern, avoid jokes that target a person's appearance or background, include classmates who are left out, and tell a trusted adult when bullying or safety concerns continue.

PEER SUPPORT

How to support a friend who is struggling

You do not need to have all the answers to show someone that you care. Often, the first step is to listen and let them know they do not have to explain everything perfectly.

You can try: “I’m glad you told me,” “Do you want me to listen or help you think through next steps?” or “Would you like us to talk to a trusted adult together?”

Respect their privacy, but do not promise to keep it secret if they may be in danger. In that situation, involve a trusted adult or professional who can help keep them safe.
